If you’re a creator—blogger, YouTuber, course seller, podcaster—you’ve probably heard of Convertkit. It’s been the darling of the creator economy for years, and in 2023 it rebranded to Kit (though most still call it ConvertKit). In 2026, the platform continues to evolve, but is it still the best email marketing tool for people who Make things online? I’ve been using ConvertKit on and off since 2020, and I’ve tested it against Mailchimp, ActiveCampaign, and even Flodesk. Here’s my full, no-fluff review.

What Is ConvertKit?

ConvertKit is an email marketing platform designed specifically for creators—people who sell digital products, run membership sites, or build audiences through content. Unlike general-purpose tools like Mailchimp, ConvertKit focuses on simplicity and audience segmentation through tags rather than lists.

It started as a simple email tool for bloggers, but over the years it’s added landing pages, forms, visual automations, and even a built-in commerce feature for selling digital products. The rebrand to “Kit” was meant to reflect its broader suite of tools, but the core remains email marketing with a creator-first twist.

Key differentiator: ConvertKit uses a tag-based system instead of traditional lists. You tag subscribers based on their interests or actions, then send targeted broadcasts or automated sequences based on those tags. This makes segmentation dead simple once you wrap your head around it.

Who Is ConvertKit For?

ConvertKit is built for solo creators and small teams who sell digital products or rely on audience engagement. It’s not great for ecommerce stores (Shopify integration is basic) or massive B2B lead generation (ActiveCampaign is better there). Here’s who I’d recommend it for:

  • Bloggers & writers – especially those with paid newsletters or courses.
  • YouTubers & podcasters – who want to grow an email list from their content.
  • Course creators – ConvertKit’s commerce features let you sell and deliver courses directly.
  • Freelancers & consultants – who need simple automations to nurture leads.
  • Small teams – with up to 5–10 people managing email campaigns.

Not ideal for:

  • Ecommerce brands (use Klaviyo or Mailchimp).
  • Large enterprises (HubSpot or ActiveCampaign).
  • Anyone who needs complex CRM functionality.

Features Deep Dive

ConvertKit’s feature set has grown significantly. Here’s what you get in 2026:

1. Tag-Based Subscriber Management

Instead of lists, you use tags to label subscribers. When someone signs up for a freebie, they get tagged “freebie-optin.” When they buy a course, they get tagged “customer.” You can then send emails only to people with specific tags. This is more flexible than lists, but it takes some getting used to.

2. Visual Automation Builder

The automation builder is clean and intuitive. You can create sequences (drip emails), set triggers (tag applied, form submitted, purchase made), and add conditions (if tag exists, do X). It’s not as powerful as ActiveCampaign’s conditionals, but for 90% of creator workflows, it’s enough.

3. Landing Pages & Forms

ConvertKit includes a drag-and-drop landing page builder and customizable forms. The templates are minimal but professional. You can embed forms on your site or use ConvertKit-hosted pages. In 2026, they added more design flexibility, but it still won’t replace a tool like Leadpages.

4. Commerce (Digital Products)

ConvertKit now lets you sell digital products directly—courses, ebooks, templates. You can set up a simple checkout page, handle payments via Stripe, and deliver content automatically. It’s not a full ecommerce solution (no upsells, no subscriptions for physical goods), but for a quick course launch, it works.

5. Subscriber Scoring

A relatively new feature: you can assign scores to subscribers based on actions (email opens, link clicks, purchases). Then use those scores to segment hot leads vs. cold ones. Handy for prioritizing who to send offers to.

6. Integrations

ConvertKit connects with most major tools via native integrations: WordPress, Shopify, Teachable, Gumroad, Zapier (natively, no Zap needed for many). The Zapier integration is robust. They also have an API for custom connections.

What We Liked

1. Simplicity for Creators

ConvertKit’s interface is clean and uncluttered. You don’t get lost in menus. The tag system, while different, becomes intuitive quickly. I can set up a welcome sequence and a landing page in under 10 minutes.

2. Excellent Deliverability

Because ConvertKit focuses on engaged senders, their deliverability rates are consistently high. I’ve never had spam issues with them, unlike Mailchimp where I’ve seen inboxing problems.

3. Free Plan for Up to 1,000 Subscribers

You get unlimited forms, landing pages, and email sends for free (with ConvertKit branding). That’s better than Mailchimp’s free plan (500 subs, limited features). For a starting creator, this is a huge win.

4. Creator-Focused Support

Their support team knows the creator space. When I asked about best practices for a course launch sequence, they gave specific advice. Not just “here’s how to set up an automation.”

5. No “List Hygiene” Hassles

ConvertKit automatically handles unsubscribes and bounces. You don’t need to manually clean lists. And they don’t charge for “cleaning” like some platforms.

What Could Be Better

1. Limited Design Flexibility

Emails are text-heavy with basic formatting. You can add images, but the templates feel like 2015. If you want beautiful, branded newsletters (like Flodesk does), ConvertKit isn’t it. It’s built for content, not design.

2. No A/B Testing (on lower plans)

A/B testing subject lines is only available on the Pro plan ($59/month). That’s a bummer for creators on the $29 plan who want to optimize open rates.

3. Commerce Is Basic

Selling digital products works, but there’s no upsell, no coupon codes, no subscription options (for recurring payments). You’ll still need Gumroad or Teachable for serious product sales.

4. Reporting Could Be Deeper

You get open rates, click rates, and subscriber growth charts. But there’s no heatmaps, no link tracking beyond clicks, and no revenue attribution (unless you integrate with Stripe manually).

5. Price Jumps at Higher Tiers

At 5,000 subscribers, the Creator plan costs $59/month. At 10,000, it’s $99/month. Compare that to Mailchimp’s Standard plan at 10k subs for $99/month (with more features). ConvertKit gets expensive fast.

Alternatives to ConvertKit

1. Mailchimp – Best for general small businesses

  • Pros: Better design, more integrations, ecommerce features.
  • Cons: Complex pricing, deliverability issues, list-based system.
  • Best for: Ecommerce stores, non-creator businesses.

2. ActiveCampaign – Best for complex automations

  • Pros: Powerful automation builder, CRM, advanced segmentation.
  • Cons: Steeper learning curve, more expensive at scale.
  • Best for: B2B, lead generation, advanced marketing teams.

3. Flodesk – Best for design-heavy emails

  • Pros: Beautiful templates, easy-to-use, flat pricing.
  • Cons: Limited features, no free plan, no commerce.
  • Best for: Creators who prioritize visual email design.

4. Moosend – Best budget option

  • Pros: Affordable, good automation, built-in landing pages.
  • Cons: Smaller user base, less creator-specific features.
  • Best for: Budget-conscious solopreneurs.

Verdict: Should You Use ConvertKit in 2026?

ConvertKit is still the best email marketing platform for creators—if you fit the mold. If you’re a blogger, course seller, or YouTuber who values deliverability and simple segmentation over fancy designs, ConvertKit is a no-brainer. The free plan gets you started, and the paid plans are reasonable up to about 5,000 subscribers.

But it’s not for everyone. If you need beautiful newsletters (go Flodesk), ecommerce (go Mailchimp), or complex B2B automations (go ActiveCampaign), look elsewhere. And if you’re price-sensitive above 5k subs, the cost jumps might push you to alternatives.

My pick: For a solo creator launching a course or building a paid newsletter, ConvertKit is the winner. For a small team managing multiple product lines, I’d lean toward ActiveCampaign.
