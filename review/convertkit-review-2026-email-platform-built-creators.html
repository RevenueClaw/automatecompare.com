ConvertKit Review 2026: The Email Platform Built for Creators

Most email tools are built for ecommerce stores or enterprise marketing departments. They want you to design glossy newsletters, track revenue attribution, and hand your list to a sales team. If you're a writer, course creator, podcaster, or freelancer, half of that is noise.

ConvertKit was built for the other crowd. And in October 2024, it changed its name to Kit — same company, same product, new logo. Plenty of people still search for "ConvertKit review," so I'll use both names throughout. Here's what actually matters: does this thing still earn its price tag in 2026, or has the competition caught up?

I've run lists on Kit, Mailchimp, MailerLite, and beehiiv. Here's my honest take.

What ConvertKit (Kit) Actually Is

Kit is an email marketing platform aimed squarely at people who Make things: writers, YouTubers, course sellers, newsletter operators, coaches, and indie consultants. Its core promise is that you can build an audience, grow it, and monetize it without stitching together five different tools.

The company has been around since 2013 and has stayed deliberately narrow. It doesn't do ecommerce email in the Klaviyo sense. It doesn't do CRM pipelines. It doesn't do SMS natively. What it does is email broadcasting, automation, landing pages, forms, and selling digital products — and it does those things well.

Who It's Built For (And Who It Isn't)

Let me save you some reading time. Kit fits you if:

  • You're a solo operator or a team of two or three
  • Your business runs on a newsletter, a course, a membership, or a digital product
  • You want automation but don't want to learn a logic-builder that looks like a flowchart from a 1990s engineering textbook
  • You care more about deliverability and simplicity than about pixel-perfect email design

Kit is probably wrong for you if:

  • You run an ecommerce store and need product feeds, browse abandonment, and revenue-per-email reporting
  • You need native SMS or a real CRM
  • You want to send heavily designed, brand-heavy HTML emails with complex layouts
  • You have a 200,000-subscriber list and a tight budget — subscriber-based pricing gets expensive

That last point is the one people underestimate. More on it below.

ConvertKit Features: A Detailed Breakdown

The Email Editor

Kit's editor is block-based. You add a text block, an image block, a button, a spacer, a divider, a video embed, a product block. You can drag blocks around and customize fonts, colors, and spacing. There's also a plain-text-friendly mode, which sounds boring until you realize plain-text emails often outperform designed ones for creator audiences.

What you won't get is a full drag-and-drop canvas like Mailchimp's or a template library with hundreds of options. Kit ships a small set of templates and expects you to build your own look. For a lot of creators that's fine — a clean, readable email with a personal tone beats a designed one. If you're a designer who wants total layout control, you'll feel the walls.

One genuinely useful detail: Kit's editor autosaves and lets you send a test to a specific segment, not just yourself. Small thing. Saves time.

Visual Automations

This is where Kit earns a lot of its reputation. The visual automation builder is a drag-and-drop canvas where you connect triggers to actions with lines. Triggers include:

  • Subscribes to a form or landing page
  • Tag added
  • Link clicked
  • Product purchased
  • Date-based (e.g., three days after signup)
  • Custom API/webhook event

Actions include sending an email, adding or removing a tag, subscribing someone to a sequence, applying a webhook, and exiting the automation. You can branch with conditions — if a subscriber has a tag, go left; if not, go right.

Compare that to ActiveCampaign, which offers goals, wait-until conditions, split testing inside automations, and dozens of triggers. Kit's automations are simpler. For 80% of creator use cases — welcome series, launch sequences, tagging buyers, re-engagement — simpler is a feature, not a bug. You'll build your first automation in twenty minutes.

The catch: if you need complex multi-branch logic with scoring-based routing, you'll hit the ceiling.

Landing Pages and Forms

Kit gives you four form types — inline, modal, slide-in, and sticky bar — plus standalone landing pages. All of them are hosted by Kit, so you don't need a website to start collecting emails. Point a subdomain at it or use the kit.com URL.

The forms are conversion-focused rather than pretty. They're fast, mobile-friendly, and easy to embed. You can create incentive emails (the "here's your free guide" delivery email) directly inside the form settings, which is a nice shortcut.

Landing pages are basic. One column, a headline, an image, a form, maybe a video. If you want a beautiful sales page, use Carrd or your own site and embed a Kit form.

The Creator Network

This is Kit's most distinctive growth feature and the one competitors keep trying to copy. It's a recommendations engine: when someone subscribes to your form, Kit shows them a list of other creators they might like, and those creators' audiences see you in return. Recommendations are free and reciprocal — you don't pay per subscriber.
