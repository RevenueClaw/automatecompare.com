Tally Review 2026: The Free Form Builder That Competes with Typeform

Most form builders Make you count responses like you're rationing coffee. Ten submissions a month on the free tier, then a paywall. For a small business collecting client intake forms or quote requests, that ceiling arrives somewhere around the second week of the month.

Tally takes the opposite approach. Unlimited forms. Unlimited submissions. Free, permanently — not a 14-day trial wearing a disguise. That's the headline, and it's the reason Tally keeps showing up in conversations about Typeform alternatives.

But "free" is a marketing claim until you actually build something with it. So I spent time building lead capture forms, a client onboarding intake, a payment-enabled booking request, and a scored quiz-style application form in Tally. This review covers what works, what doesn't, and where the free plan quietly runs out of road.

What Tally Actually Is

Tally is a form builder built around a block editor — think Notion's slash-command system applied to forms. You type / and pick a block: short text, email, multiple choice, file upload, payment, whatever you need. You can rearrange blocks by dragging, and the whole thing renders as either a classic form or a conversational one-question-at-a-time layout.

The company is Belgian, founded by Marie Martens and Filip Minev, and the product has grown mostly through word of mouth among freelancers, agencies, and Notion-heavy teams. The pitch is simple: Typeform-style forms without Typeform-style response limits or pricing.

That's the theory. Here's how it holds up.

Feature Breakdown

The Block Editor

This is Tally's strongest design decision. Instead of dragging fields into a grid and fiddling with column widths, you build forms the way you'd write a document. Type /email and an email field appears. Type /payment and you get a Stripe payment block. Add a heading, drop in a paragraph of explanatory text between questions, insert a divider.

The practical benefit shows up when you're editing an existing form. Need to move question 7 above question 4? Cut and paste the block. Need to add a paragraph explaining why you're asking for a phone number? Drop it in. In Typeform, that kind of structural edit is more tedious — you're clicking into a question, dragging handles, and hoping the layout holds.

The tradeoff: Tally's editor is text-first. If you want pixel-precise control over spacing, background images behind individual questions, or elaborate multi-column layouts, you'll fight it. It's a form builder for people who think in documents, not designers who think in canvases.

Question Types

The standard set is all there: short text, long text, email, phone number, number, URL, date, time, dropdown, multiple choice, checkboxes, linear scale, rating, file upload, and payment. Tally also supports hidden fields, which is more useful than it sounds — you can pass a UTM parameter or a client ID through the URL and store it silently with each submission. That's the kind of thing that makes form-to-CRM automation actually work instead of producing a pile of unattributed leads.

Signature fields exist but are less polished than what you'd get in Jotform. If signed documents are core to your workflow — contracts, waivers, consent forms — Jotform's signature and PDF-generation stack is deeper.

Logic and Calculations

Conditional logic is available on the free plan, which is worth calling out because Typeform gates similar functionality behind paid tiers. You can show or hide questions based on earlier answers, and jump respondents to different sections.

Answer piping works too — pull a name from question 1 into the thank-you message on the last page. For client intake forms, this is the difference between "Thanks for submitting" and "Thanks, Sarah — we'll review your project details and reply within one business day." Small thing, real difference in how the form feels.

Calculated fields are where Tally gets interesting for quoting. You can build a form that asks for quantity, service type, and add-ons, then computes an estimated price and displays it to the respondent. For a freelancer or small agency, that's a lightweight quoting tool without building a spreadsheet calculator or buying a CPQ product.

The limit: calculations and advanced logic sit behind the Pro tier. Free covers basic show/hide and jumps; if your form needs to do math, budget for Pro.

Payments via Stripe

Tally connects directly to Stripe. You add a payment block, set a fixed amount or a variable one tied to a calculated field, and the form collects payment at submission. No redirect to a separate checkout page, no third-party payment widget bolted on.

For a small business taking deposits on project bookings or selling event tickets, this removes a step. The money lands in your Stripe account, the submission lands in your Tally workspace, and your automation picks it up from there.
