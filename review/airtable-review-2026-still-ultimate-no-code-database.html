Airtable Review 2026: Is It Still the Ultimate No-Code Database?

Airtable has been the default answer to "where should this data live?" for about a decade now. Spreadsheet too simple, real database too complicated, and you need something in between that a marketing coordinator can actually use without filing a ticket with IT. That's the gap Airtable filled, and for a long time it filled it alone.

That's not the situation in 2026. Notion's databases got considerably better. Baserow and NocoDB matured into genuine contenders. Microsoft Lists is bundled into plenty of business subscriptions. And Airtable itself has changed — new AI features, a new app-building product called Omni, reshuffled plans, and pricing that has crept upward year after year.

So the question worth asking isn't whether Airtable is good. It's whether it's still worth the money for a small team. I've spent the last few weeks rebuilding real workflows in it — a content calendar, a lightweight CRM, and an inventory tracker — to find out. Here's what I found.

What Airtable Actually Is

Strip away the marketing and Airtable is a relational database with a spreadsheet skin. You create a base, which is roughly what you'd call a database. Inside a base you have tables — think sheets, except each one is a proper collection of records. Tables can link to each other, which is the part that separates Airtable from Google Sheets in any meaningful way.

A quick example. Say you run a small design studio. You'd have a Clients table, a Projects table, and an Invoices table. Each project links to a client. Each invoice links to a project. Change a client's name once and it updates everywhere. Try doing that in a spreadsheet without a pile of VLOOKUPs that break the moment someone inserts a row.

That relational core is why people call it a no-code database rather than a spreadsheet. It's also why switching away from Airtable gets painful once you've built a few linked tables.

Airtable Features, Broken Down

The Grid and Field Types

The grid is where you'll spend most of your time, and it behaves the way you'd hope. Paste from Excel or Google Sheets and it maps columns sensibly. Drag to reorder. Group, filter, sort, and hide fields per view rather than globally — which sounds minor until you've used a tool that doesn't do it.

Field types are where the depth shows. Beyond text, number, and date, you get:

  • Linked records — the relational backbone
  • Lookup and rollup fields — pull values from linked tables, then sum, average, or concatenate them
  • Formula fields — a decent formula language with functions for dates, text, logic, and arrays
  • Attachments — files, images, and previews stored on the record
  • Single select and multi-select — with color coding that actually helps scanning
  • Checkbox, rating, duration, currency, percent
  • Barcode — scan with a phone camera, useful for inventory
  • Button — trigger an automation, open a URL, or update a record with one tap
  • AI fields — more on these below
  • User and collaborator fields — assign records to actual people

There are around 30 field types at this point. Most small businesses use maybe eight of them.

Views

One table, many views. That's the core idea, and it's still Airtable's strongest trick.

Views aren't just display options. Each one carries its own filters, sorts, groupings, field visibility, and even its own color rules. A sales rep and a warehouse manager can look at the same table and see completely different things. That's genuinely useful and it's the reason Airtable replaced three separate tools for a lot of teams.

Interfaces

Views are for people inside the base. Interfaces are for everyone else — the client who shouldn't see your margin column, the contractor who only needs to log hours, the exec who wants a dashboard and nothing more.

You drag together a layout, pick which records feed it, and share a link. Interfaces can include charts, record pickers, buttons, and filtered lists. On the Business plan and above you can build quite elaborate ones.

This is the feature that most often justifies the price jump from Team to Business. If you're building something clients will log into, you need it.

Automations

Airtable's built-in automation builder handles the usual pattern: when this happens, do that. Triggers include record created, record matches conditions, form submitted, scheduled time, and webhook received. Actions include update record, create record, send email, send Slack message, run a script, and call an external API.

The practical ceiling is run count. The Free plan gives you 1,000 automation runs a month, which a real workflow will chew through in a week. Team gets 25,000. Business gets 100,000. If you're triggering an automation on every record update in a busy base, watch that number closely — it's the most common surprise on a bill.

For anything more complex than a few steps, you'll want Zapier or Make alongside it. Airtable's native builder is fine for internal logic; multi-app orchestration is better handled elsewhere.

AI Features

Airtable has leaned hard into AI. There are AI-powered fields that summarize text, categorize records, translate, and extract structured data from messy input. There are AI steps inside automations. And there's Omni, Airtable's separate app-building product that generates working applications from a prompt plus your data.

The AI fields are the part most small teams will actually use. Point one at a "Notes" field and have it extract a next action and a due date. Point another at customer feedback and have it tag sentiment. It works reasonably well, though you should still spot-check output on anything customer-facing.

AI usage is metered by credits, and the credit allowances on each plan are generous enough for light use but not for running AI across every row of a 50,000-record table. Read the credit terms before you build a workflow that depends on them.

Integrations and Extensions

Airtable connects natively to Slack, Google Drive, Google Calendar, Outlook, Jira, Salesforce, Zendesk, GitHub, and a long list of others. Beyond native integrations, Zapier and Make both support Airtable well, and webhooks cover the rest.

Extensions sit inside the base and add functionality without leaving it — maps, charts, pivot tables, QR code generators, and a scripting panel. Some are made by Airtable, many by third parties. Quality varies, and the extension marketplace is less curated than it used to be.

Sync, API, and Scripting

Sync tables pull data from another base or an external source on a schedule and keep it updated. Useful for a reporting base that mirrors operational data without duplicating work.

The REST API is well documented and works fine. The scripting extension runs JavaScript against your base, which is the escape hatch when the formula language and automation builder both come up short. If your team has one person who can write a bit of JS, you can extend Airtable a long way.

Collaboration and Permissions

Comment threads, @mentions, revision history, and granular sharing. You can share a base with a specific person, share a view publicly, share a form, or share an interface. Enterprise plans add SSO, audit logs, and admin controls.

One caveat: Airtable's permission model is base-level and interface-level, not row-level. If you need different customers to see different subsets of records in the same table, you're building that with interfaces and filters, or you're looking at Enterprise.

A few things worth flagging that the table doesn't capture:

  • Free is capped at 5 seats. Fine for testing, not for a team.
  • Business is where interfaces, advanced sync, and AI credits live. Most small businesses that get real value out of Airtable end up here, and $45 per seat adds up fast. Ten users is $5,400 a year.
  • Enterprise requires a conversation with sales. No public price, and it's not cheap.
  • Prices have moved. If you're comparing against a quote from two years ago, expect a difference. Airtable changes pricing more often than most competitors.

For a five-person team on Business, you're looking at roughly $2,700 annually. That's the number to weigh against Baserow at $5–$11 per user or self-hosted NocoDB at zero.
