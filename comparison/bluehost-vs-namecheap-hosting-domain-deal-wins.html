Bluehost vs Namecheap: Which Hosting or Domain Deal Wins in 2026?

Here's the thing nobody tells you when you start comparing these two: Bluehost and Namecheap aren't really the same kind of company. They just both sell you the same two things.

Bluehost started as a hosting company and bolted on domain registration later. Namecheap started as a domain registrar and bolted on hosting later. That origin story shapes everything — the pricing, the support, the upsells, and which one is actually the better buy for what you're doing.

So the honest answer to "which wins?" is: it depends on whether you're shopping for hosting or a domain. And for a lot of small business owners, it's both.

Let me walk through where each one is strong, where each one will nickel-and-dime you, and what I'd actually pick.

The 30-Second Version

  • Need a domain name? Namecheap. Free WHOIS privacy, lower renewals, no nonsense.
  • Need WordPress hosting with training wheels? Bluehost, mostly because of the phone support and the WordPress.org relationship.
  • Want the lowest total cost over three years? Namecheap, and it's not close once Bluehost's renewal prices kick in.
  • Want one dashboard for everything and don't mind paying for it? Bluehost.

Now the details.

Hosting: Where Bluehost Has the Home Advantage

Bluehost's shared hosting lineup has been roughly the same for years: Basic, Choice Plus, Online Store, and Pro. Every plan includes a free domain for the first year, a free SSL certificate, and a Cloudflare CDN.

The thing that genuinely separates Bluehost from most budget hosts is support. You can call them. Twenty-four hours a day, on the phone, and reach a human who can walk you through pointing a DNS record or restoring a backup. Namecheap doesn't offer phone support at all — it's live chat and tickets only.

For a solo operator who's never touched a hosting control panel, that phone line is worth real money. It's the single biggest reason Bluehost keeps showing up on "best web hosting 2026" lists despite middling performance scores.

Namecheap's shared hosting is called Stellar, and it's positioned as the budget option — because it is one. Performance, honestly

Neither of these is a performance host. If you're running a store doing six figures, you should be on Cloudways, Kinsta, or a VPS — not on a $3 shared plan from either company.

That said, there are differences worth knowing:

  • Bluehost puts a custom caching layer on Choice Plus and above and leans on Cloudflare for its CDN. Independent uptime monitors have generally clocked it around 99.9%, with occasional dips. Time-to-first-byte on shared plans tends to be on the slower side.
  • Namecheap Stellar runs on SSD storage with LiteSpeed on newer infrastructure and includes a CDN on Plus and Business. It's competitive with Bluehost, sometimes a bit faster, sometimes not — the honest answer is that results vary by server and by month.

If page speed is your deciding factor, don't pick based on marketing copy. Pick based on a host with a money-back window, test your actual site, and leave if it's slow. Both give you 30 days.

Domains: Where Namecheap Runs Away With It

This is where the comparison stops being close.

Namecheap is a domain registrar first. It sells domains at close to wholesale, includes WHOIS privacy protection for free, forever, on every eligible domain, and doesn't try to sell you six add-ons at checkout. Free DNS management, free email forwarding, and a clean dashboard that lets you point a domain somewhere in about ninety seconds.

Bluehost gives you a free domain for the first year when you buy hosting. Sounds great. Then:

  • Renewal pricing is higher. A .com that renews around $17 at Namecheap will generally cost you more at Bluehost.
  • WHOIS privacy is an add-on. Namecheap includes it. Bluehost has historically charged for it.
  • The domain is tied to the hosting. If you move hosts later, you have to transfer the domain out, which is an extra step people forget about until it bites them.

Here's the move I recommend to basically every small business owner: buy your domain at Namecheap, buy your hosting wherever you want. They don't have to be the same company, and keeping them separate means you can switch hosts without touching your domain. You just update two nameservers.
