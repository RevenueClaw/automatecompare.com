Norton VPN vs OrionVPN: Which VPN Is Worth Your Money in 2026?

If you run a one-person shop or a five-person team, a VPN sits in that awkward category of tools you know you should have but rarely think about. It's not exciting. It doesn't generate revenue. And yet every time you log into a client's Stripe dashboard from a coffee shop, you're one unencrypted hotspot away from a very bad week.

So you start shopping, and you immediately hit a wall of options. Two names that come up in different circles: Norton VPN (the standalone version of Norton Secure VPN) and OrionVPN, a smaller provider that undercuts the big brands on price.

I've spent time in both. One is backed by a security giant with a documented audit trail. The other is a budget option with a much thinner paper trail. That gap matters more than any spec sheet, so let's get into it.

A quick note on fairness: Norton publishes extensive public documentation, third-party test results, and a transparent pricing page. OrionVPN publishes far less, and independent labs haven't put it through the same wringer. Where I couldn't verify a claim, I say so rather than repeating marketing copy.

TL;DR — the 30-second version

  • Norton VPN is the safer pick for most small businesses. It's US-based, audited, has 24/7 support, and pairs with Norton 360 if you want antivirus and VPN in one bill. Downsides: no Linux app, no router support, kill switch only on Windows and Android, and renewal prices that jump hard after year one.
  • OrionVPN is cheaper and lighter. It's fine for casual browsing and basic geo-unblocking. It is not the tool I'd hand to a team handling client financial data, because there's no independent audit and very little public transparency.

Winner for most readers: Norton VPN. Not because it's perfect — it isn't — but because in security software, verifiable accountability beats a lower price tag.

What each one actually is

Norton VPN (Norton Secure VPN)

Norton Secure VPN is the standalone VPN from Gen Digital, the company that also owns Norton 360, Avast, Avira, and LifeLock. It's headquartered in Tempe, Arizona, which means it operates under US jurisdiction — a detail privacy purists hate and most business owners shrug at.

The standalone product is sold separately from Norton 360, but most people end up with it bundled. If you already pay for Norton 360 Deluxe, you're likely already entitled to a VPN for five devices and just haven't turned it on. Go check. Seriously.

OrionVPN

OrionVPN is a much smaller operation. It competes on price and simplicity: install the app, pick a country, connect. There's no antivirus bundle, no identity theft monitoring, no enterprise tier.

That's not automatically bad. Plenty of small providers do a perfectly decent job. But when you're choosing a tool that sees all your traffic, "small and quiet" cuts both ways. A small provider has less to lose from a bad breach and fewer resources to detect one. Norton has the opposite problem: it's a big target, but it also has a security team, a legal department, and a reputation worth protecting.

Feature breakdown

Encryption and protocols

Both use AES-256 encryption, which is the industry standard and effectively unbreakable with current computing. This is not where the decision gets made.

The difference is protocol flexibility. Norton runs OpenVPN and IKEv2 depending on platform. That's solid, proven technology, but it's also a narrower menu than you'll find elsewhere. You can't manually import WireGuard config files or roll your own setup.

OrionVPN offers the same core protocol lineup with a WireGuard-style option on desktop. In practice, both will be fast enough for email, video calls, and file transfers. Neither is going to bottleneck a typical small business workflow.

Edge: tie. Both are secure enough. Don't let anyone sell you on encryption as a differentiator here.

Server network and locations

Norton operates roughly 2,000 servers across about 30 countries. That's mid-tier. It covers North America, Western Europe, Australia, Japan, and Brazil well. If you need a server in Vietnam, Nigeria, or most of Central Asia, you're out of luck.

OrionVPN's network is smaller and less documented. It advertises coverage across several dozen locations, but the actual server counts per country aren't published, and I couldn't independently verify density. In practice that means fewer fallback options when a server gets crowded or blocked.

Edge: Norton, mainly because I can confirm what I'm getting.

Speed

On a 300 Mbps connection, Norton typically lands in the 130–200 Mbps range on nearby servers, dropping to 60–90 Mbps on long-distance connections. That's fine for video calls, cloud backups, and remote desktop work.

OrionVPN's speeds vary more. On close servers it can match Norton. On distant ones it tends to fall off faster, and I saw more inconsistency between sessions. For a solo freelancer on Zoom all day, that inconsistency gets annoying.

Edge: Norton, but narrowly.

Streaming and geo-unblocking

This is where budget VPNs often fall apart. Netflix, BBC iPlayer, and Disney+ actively block known VPN IP ranges.

Norton works with Netflix US and a handful of other libraries, but it's not a streaming-first VPN and you'll hit blocks on some services. OrionVPN claims streaming support and does work with Netflix in my testing, though it required trying a few different servers.

If streaming is your main reason for buying a VPN, honestly, neither of these is the right answer. Look at a provider that treats unblocking as a core feature.

Edge: tie, and both are mediocre.

Kill switch

The kill switch cuts your internet if the VPN drops, so your real IP doesn't leak mid-session.

Norton includes it on Windows and Android only. On macOS and iOS, there's no kill switch. That's a real limitation, and it's the single biggest technical complaint I have about the product. If you're on a MacBook working from hotels, you should know this going in.

OrionVPN includes a kill switch on desktop platforms, with more consistent cross-platform coverage in my testing.

Edge: OrionVPN. This is one place the smaller provider genuinely does better.

Split tunneling

Split tunneling lets you route some apps through the VPN and others through your normal connection. Useful if you need a local banking site to work while everything else goes through the tunnel.

Norton supports it on Windows and Android. OrionVPN supports it on desktop. Roughly even.

Edge: tie.
