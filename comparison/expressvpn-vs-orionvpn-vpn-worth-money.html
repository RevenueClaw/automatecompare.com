ExpressVPN vs OrionVPN: Which VPN Is Worth Your Money in 2026?

Most VPN comparisons pit two giants against each other and call it a day. This one is different. ExpressVPN is the household name with the audits, the server count, and the price tag to match. OrionVPN is the scrappy alternative that keeps showing up in budget VPN roundups — cheaper, quieter, and much harder to verify.

That gap matters more than any spec sheet. If you're a freelancer logging into client dashboards from airport Wi-Fi, or a small business owner paying for five seats, the question isn't "which one has more servers." It's "which one am I still comfortable using in year three?"

So I dug into both. Here's what actually separates them.

A note on sourcing: ExpressVPN publishes detailed technical documentation, third-party audit reports, and transparent pricing. OrionVPN publishes far less, and independent testing of its apps is thin. Where I couldn't verify a claim myself, I've said so instead of repeating marketing copy as fact. Treat anything marked "claimed" as exactly that.

The short version

  • Pick ExpressVPN if you want verified no-logs audits, a large server network, and support that answers in under two minutes. You'll pay roughly two to three times more.
  • Pick OrionVPN if your budget is the binding constraint and your threat model is "coffee shop Wi-Fi, not state surveillance." Go in with open eyes about the transparency gap.

Now the details.

What each one actually is

ExpressVPN launched in 2009 and has been one of the most recognized names in consumer VPNs ever since. It's headquartered in the British Virgin Islands and has been owned by Kape Technologies since 2021 — a detail worth knowing, since Kape also owns CyberGhost, PIA, and ZenMate. ExpressVPN operates a network of RAM-only servers, runs its own proprietary protocol called Lightway, and has paid for multiple independent audits of its infrastructure and no-logs claims.

OrionVPN is a smaller, lesser-known provider that markets itself primarily on price and simplicity. You won't find a published audit from a firm like Cure53 or KPMG. You won't find a detailed transparency report. What you will find is a lower monthly number and a straightforward app.

That's not automatically a red flag. Small VPN companies exist all over the place, and plenty of them are honest. But "we don't log" is a promise, not a proof. ExpressVPN pays real money to have someone else check. OrionVPN, as far as I can tell, does not.

Feature breakdown

Server network and locations

ExpressVPN runs roughly 3,000 servers across 105 countries. For most people that's more than enough, and it means you can usually find a nearby server rather than routing through a single overloaded hub in the Netherlands. It also matters for geo-testing: if you need to check how a client's landing page renders in Japan, Brazil, and Germany, you have options.

OrionVPN's network is considerably smaller. The company advertises servers in a few dozen locations, concentrated in North America and Europe, with thinner coverage in Asia and almost nothing in Africa or South America. If you mostly connect to servers in the US or UK, this is fine. If you need a specific country for work, check the list before you pay.

Edge: ExpressVPN, clearly.

Speed and protocols

ExpressVPN's Lightway protocol is the real differentiator here. It's built on a lightweight codebase, connects in a couple of seconds, and holds up well on flaky hotel Wi-Fi where OpenVPN sometimes stalls. In late 2024 ExpressVPN added post-quantum protection to Lightway, which is a forward-looking move for anyone handling sensitive client data.

OrionVPN supports WireGuard and IKEv2, which is a perfectly reasonable protocol lineup. WireGuard is fast — often faster than Lightway on paper. The catch is implementation quality. A well-tuned WireGuard setup beats a mediocre proprietary protocol; a poorly maintained one drops connections and leaks. Independent speed testing of OrionVPN is scarce, so I can't tell you how it performs under load across a full day of video calls.

Edge: ExpressVPN on consistency and documentation. OrionVPN might match it on raw throughput, but I can't confirm that.

Privacy, logging, and audits

This is where the two diverge most sharply.

ExpressVPN has undergone independent audits of its TrustedServer technology and its no-logs policy, including work from Cure53 and KPMG. Its servers run entirely in RAM, meaning a reboot wipes everything. It's based in the BVI, outside the Five Eyes, Nine Eyes, and Fourteen Eyes intelligence alliances. None of that makes it perfect, and the Kape ownership is a legitimate concern for some buyers, but the claims are at least checkable.

OrionVPN states that it doesn't keep activity logs. That's the standard line. What's missing is the third-party verification, a published warrant canary, or any detail about how the servers are configured. The company may well be telling the truth. You just have no independent way to confirm it.

For a solo freelancer sending invoices, this gap is probably acceptable. For a business handling client financial records or healthcare data, it's a dealbreaker.

Edge: ExpressVPN, and it's not close.

Device support and simultaneous connections

ExpressVPN allows 8 simultaneous connections on a single account, with apps for Windows, macOS, Linux, iOS, Android, smart TVs, routers, and browser extensions. There's also a router app if you'd rather cover every device in the office with one connection.

OrionVPN typically advertises a similar or slightly higher device count, and its app coverage is narrower — desktop and mobile, with less polish on TV and router setups. If you're running a small team and want to expense one account, 8 devices covers a laptop, phone, tablet, and a couple of office machines without much strain.

Edge: Roughly even on paper. ExpressVPN wins on app quality and router support.

Business-specific features

ExpressVPN has offered a Teams plan with centralized billing and an admin console, though the exact packaging has shifted over the years — confirm current availability if you need seat management. It also accepts a wider range of payment methods, including credit cards and PayPal, and provides invoices that are easy to hand to an accountant.

OrionVPN's business offering is essentially "buy more accounts." There's no admin dashboard, no centralized user management, and limited payment flexibility. For a two-person operation, that's irrelevant. For a ten-person agency, it's a headache.

Edge: ExpressVPN.

Support

ExpressVPN's 24/7 live chat typically connects you to a human in under two minutes. I've used it. It's one of the better support experiences in the category, and they'll walk you through router configuration without making you feel stupid.

OrionVPN's support is email-based with slower response times and no published 24/7 commitment. For a tool you rely on to reach client systems, that's a meaningful risk.

Edge: ExpressVPN.

Pros and cons

ExpressVPN

Pros

  • Independently audited no-logs policy, with published reports you can actually read
  • Lightway connects fast and stays connected on unreliable networks
  • 105 countries means you can almost always find a nearby server
  • 8 simultaneous devices covers a small team on one account
  • 24/7 live chat that resolves real problems, including router setups
  • 30-day money-back guarantee gives you a genuine test period
  • Post-quantum encryption for future-proofing

Cons

  • Costs two to three times more than budget competitors
  • Owned by Kape Technologies, which some privacy-minded buyers dislike
  • The 8-device limit is tight if you're covering a larger office
  • No dedicated IP option on standard plans in most regions

OrionVPN

Pros

  • Cheapest entry point of the two, especially on annual plans
  • WireGuard support means decent speeds when it works well
  • Simple apps with few settings to get lost in
  • Fine for casual use: public Wi-Fi, travel, basic geo-testing
