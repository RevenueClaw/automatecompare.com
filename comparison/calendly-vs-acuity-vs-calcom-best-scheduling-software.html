Calendly vs Acuity vs Cal.com: Best Scheduling Software Compared

Every scheduling tool promises to end the "what time works for you?" email chain. Most of them do. The problem is that Calendly, Acuity, and Cal.com solve that problem in three genuinely different ways — and picking the wrong one means paying for features you'll never touch while missing the one you actually need.

Here's the short version before we get into the details:

  • Calendly is the safest bet if you're booking sales calls, demos, or consultations and you want it working in ten minutes.
  • Acuity Scheduling wins for service businesses — salons, therapists, trainers, tutors — that need classes, packages, memberships, and proper client records.
  • Cal.com is the pick for developers, privacy-minded operators, and anyone who wants to self-host or embed scheduling inside their own product.

Now let's look at why.

What Actually Matters When You're Choosing

I've set up scheduling for a one-person consulting shop, a three-person therapy practice, and a small agency with a rotating sales team. The tool that fits depends on four questions:

  • Are you booking people, or resources? Calendly thinks in terms of people and calendars. Acuity thinks in terms of services and staff. Cal.com thinks in terms of events and APIs.
  • Do clients pay at booking? If yes, Acuity's checkout flow is more developed than Calendly's.
  • Do you need client records? Acuity keeps intake forms, notes, and history in one place. Calendly treats each booking as a meeting, not a client relationship.
  • How much control do you want over the code? Only Cal.com lets you take the whole thing and run it yourself.

Let's go through the features one at a time.

Booking Pages and Setup

Calendly is still the easiest to get running. You connect a calendar, pick your availability, and share a link. That's it. The default booking page looks clean without any customization, which matters if you're sending it to prospects who judge you on it.

The trade-off is flexibility. Calendly's booking page customization is limited to colors, logos, and a few layout choices. You can't build a custom intake flow, and you can't do much beyond basic questions unless you move up to a paid tier.

Acuity takes longer to configure because there's more to configure. You're setting up appointment types, durations, buffers, staff, and service categories. For a single consultant, that's overkill. For a salon with four stylists and twelve services, it's exactly right — and the setup time pays for itself in the first week.

Acuity's embeddable scheduler is the best of the three for putting on your own site. It can be styled to match your brand, and the Squarespace integration (Squarespace owns Acuity) is one-click if you're on that platform.

Cal.com sits in the middle. Setup is quick, the booking page is customizable, and the open-source nature means if something bugs you, someone has probably already built a fix. The interface is less polished than Calendly's in places — you'll notice small rough edges — but it's improved a lot and it's genuinely pleasant to use now.

Calendar Sync and Availability Rules

All three handle the basics: two-way sync with Google Calendar, Outlook, and Apple Calendar, plus buffer times and daily booking limits.

Where they separate:

  • Calendly handles multiple calendar connections well and lets you check for conflicts across them. You get one calendar connection on the free plan and up to six on Standard.
  • Acuity has the most granular availability rules. You can set different hours per staff member, per service, and per day, and layer in "class" schedules alongside one-on-one appointments. If your availability is complicated, Acuity handles it.
  • Cal.com is the most flexible in a technical sense — you can define availability via API, and the round-robin and collective logic is configurable in ways the others aren't.

One thing worth flagging: Calendly's free plan only allows one calendar connection, which breaks the moment you keep work and personal calendars separate. That's the single most common reason people upgrade.

Payments at Booking

Acuity is the clear winner here. It supports Stripe, PayPal, Square, and Authorize.net, and it handles the full commerce layer: deposits, full prepayment, no-show fees, gift certificates, coupons, and recurring memberships. If you run a yoga studio or a dog-grooming business, Acuity's payment stack is the reason to pick it.

Calendly does payments through Stripe and PayPal on paid plans. You can require payment or a deposit at booking. It works, but it's a booking feature bolted on, not a commerce system. There are no packages, no memberships, no gift certificates.

Cal.com supports Stripe payments on paid plans. It's functional and improving, but it's the thinnest of the three. If money changing hands is central to your workflow, Cal.com isn't there yet.

Intake Forms and Client Management

This is where Acuity pulls away from the pack.

Acuity lets you build detailed intake forms with file uploads, conditional logic, and required fields — the kind of thing a law firm or clinic needs before a first appointment. Client records persist across bookings, so you can see history, notes, and past forms. You can also sell packages that track remaining sessions.

Calendly offers custom questions on paid plans and file uploads on higher tiers. But each booking is essentially standalone. There's no client profile that accumulates over time. For sales calls, that's fine — your CRM holds the relationship. For a therapy practice, it's a gap.

Cal.com has booking questions and basic form fields. It's closer to Calendly than Acuity here, though the API means you can pipe responses anywhere you want.

Group Events, Classes, and Team Scheduling

Calendly's Teams plan adds round-robin distribution (great for sales teams sharing inbound leads), collective availability (find a time when three people are all free), and group events with seat limits. Routing forms let you send prospects to the right person based on their answers — genuinely useful if you have a qualification step.

Acuity handles classes and group appointments natively, including capacity limits, waitlists, and recurring class schedules. It also supports up to 36 staff on the Powerhouse plan. If you're running workshops or group sessions alongside one-on-ones, Acuity is the only one of the three built for that from the ground up.

Cal.com offers round-robin, collective, and seated events on its Teams plan. The logic is solid and the pricing per seat is competitive. It also lets you set up "managed" event types where one person books on behalf of others — handy for agencies and recruiters.
