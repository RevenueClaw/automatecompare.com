Notion vs Coda vs Airtable: Which All-in-One Workspace Actually Fits Your Business?

Most small teams end up with the same mess. A Notion doc for the roadmap. A spreadsheet for the client tracker. A Trello board nobody updates. A Google Doc for meeting notes. Four subscriptions, four logins, and a nagging feeling that half your information is in the wrong place.

The pitch behind Notion, Coda, and Airtable is that you can collapse all of that into one tool. That pitch is mostly true — but the three tools get there in very different ways, and picking the wrong one means months of fighting your own workspace.

Here's what actually matters when you're choosing.

The short version for people who hate long intros

  • Notion is a document tool that learned to do databases. Best for writing-heavy teams, wikis, and general company knowledge.
  • Coda is a document tool that learned to do spreadsheets. Best for teams who want logic, buttons, and interactive docs without leaving the page.
  • Airtable is a database that learned to look friendly. Best for structured data, pipelines, and anything that needs to scale past a few thousand rows.

What each tool actually is

Notion

Notion started as a note-taking app and grew outward. Everything in it is a "block" — a paragraph, a heading, an image, a database row. You can nest blocks inside blocks, turn any page into a database, and link databases together with relations and rollups.

The result is a tool that feels like a blank canvas. That's the appeal and the problem. Notion gives you almost no opinion about structure, so a well-organized workspace and a chaotic one look identical on day one.

Notion has also expanded well past docs. There's Notion Calendar, Notion Mail, Notion Sites for publishing pages as websites, and Notion Forms for collecting responses straight into a database.

Coda

Coda takes the document and makes it run code. You write in it like a doc, but any table inside that doc can have formulas, buttons, and automations wired to it. You can build a project tracker where clicking "Mark complete" updates three other tables and sends a Slack message — all from inside the page.

Coda's formula language is closer to a real spreadsheet than Notion's. It has hundreds of functions, named formulas you can reuse, and something called Packs — integrations built by Coda and third parties that add new functions to the formula language itself.

The tradeoff: Coda docs can get complicated fast. A doc with twenty tables and forty buttons is genuinely hard to hand off to a new hire.

Airtable

Airtable is a relational database with a spreadsheet face. Records live in tables, tables link to each other, and you view the same data through grid, calendar, kanban, gallery, timeline, or form views.

Where Airtable pulls ahead is structure. Field types are strict — a date field is a date field, not a text field that happens to contain a date. Linked records behave like real foreign keys. Rollups and lookups work the way you'd expect if you've ever used SQL.

Airtable also has Interfaces, which let you build simple internal apps on top of your data — a client portal, a dashboard, an intake form — without exposing the underlying base.

Feature breakdown

Documents and long-form writing

Notion wins this one, and it isn't close. The editor is genuinely pleasant for long documents. Headings, toggles, callouts, columns, synced blocks, table of contents, nested pages — it's built for writing first.

Coda's editor is good but clearly secondary to its logic layer. You can write long docs in it, and the ability to embed live tables mid-sentence is useful, but formatting gets fiddly.

Airtable is not a writing tool. You get a long text field and a rich text field. That's it. If your team writes documentation, proposals, or SOPs, Airtable will frustrate everyone.

Databases and views

All three have databases. The differences are in limits and structure.

Notion databases are flexible but shallow. You get table, board, calendar, timeline, gallery, and list views. Relations and rollups work. But Notion gets sluggish past a few thousand rows in a single database, and it has no real concept of field validation. You can't stop someone from typing "next tuesday" into a date field.

Coda tables live inside a doc, so they're scoped to that doc unless you use cross-doc sync. That's fine for project trackers and terrible for a company-wide CRM with 50,000 contacts.

Airtable is built for scale. A Team plan base handles 50,000 records, Business pushes to 125,000, and Enterprise goes higher. Field types are enforced. Linked records, rollups, and lookups are first-class features rather than add-ons.

Formulas

This is where Coda quietly beats both competitors.

Notion's Formula 2.0 is decent now — you can do conditionals, date math, and string manipulation. But it's still limited compared to a spreadsheet, and debugging a broken Notion formula is a miserable experience.

Coda's formula language has hundreds of functions, supports named formulas, and lets you write logic that spans multiple tables in one expression. If you've ever wanted a doc that calculates commissions or prorates a retainer, Coda handles it without a separate spreadsheet.

Airtable's formulas are spreadsheet-like and reliable, but they operate on a single record at a time. Cross-table math means rollups or automations, not a formula.

Automations

All three can trigger actions when something changes.

Notion's database automations are the newest and simplest. You can trigger on a status change or a date, then send a Slack message, create a page, or edit a property. Buttons let you run a sequence manually. It covers the basics and stops there.

Coda's automations are more capable — rule-based and time-based triggers, multi-step sequences, and the ability to run arbitrary formulas as an action. The catch is that Coda docs get fragile as automations pile up. One broken reference and half your doc stops working.

Airtable's automations are the most predictable. Triggers on record creation, updates, form submissions, or schedules. Actions include updating records, sending emails, calling webhooks, and running scripts. Run limits are the thing to watch — the Free plan caps you at roughly 100 runs per month per base, Team jumps to around 25,000, and Business to about 100,000.

AI features

Notion AI is an add-on at roughly $8 per seat per month on annual billing. It summarizes pages, drafts text, answers questions across your workspace, and can auto-fill database properties. The workspace-wide search is the genuinely useful part.

Coda AI is bundled into paid plans rather than sold separately. It writes, summarizes, and can fill table columns from a prompt — which sounds gimmicky until you use it to categorize 400 support tickets.

Airtable AI is an add-on too, around $6 per seat per month annually. It's aimed at classification, summarization, and generating records. Paired with Airtable's structure, it's the most useful of the three for data cleanup work.

Collaboration and permissions

Notion's permissions are page-level and easy to understand. You can share a single page with a client without giving them the rest of the workspace. Guest seats are limited on lower plans.

Coda has page-level locking and granular sharing, plus the ability to publish a doc as a read-only site. Good for client-facing dashboards.

Airtable's permissions are the most granular — you can restrict access at the base, table, field, and even view level, and Interfaces let you expose only what you want. For anything involving external parties or sensitive data, this matters.
