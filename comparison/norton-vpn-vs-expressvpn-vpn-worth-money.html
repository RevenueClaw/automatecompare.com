Norton VPN vs ExpressVPN: Which VPN Is Worth Your Money in 2026?

If you work from a laptop that travels — coffee shops, coworking desks, hotel Wi-Fi, the airport gate you swore you'd never use for client calls — a VPN stops being a nice-to-have and starts being part of your stack. The problem is that the two names you see most often, Norton and ExpressVPN, come at the problem from completely different directions.

Norton built its VPN as a side dish to a security suite. ExpressVPN built its entire company around the tunnel. That difference shows up in the server count, the speed tests, the refund window, and — most painfully — in what you pay in year two.

I've run both on the same Windows laptop, the same iPhone, and the same home router setup. Here's what actually matters when you're deciding between them.

The Short Answer

Pick ExpressVPN if you care about speed, streaming, torrenting, or a privacy policy that's been audited repeatedly by outside firms. It costs more, but you know what you're getting.

Pick Norton VPN if you already want Norton 360 for antivirus, password management, and dark web monitoring — and you'd rather fold the VPN into one bill than juggle two subscriptions. The VPN alone is a weaker product, but the bundle math is genuinely good in year one.

Now let's get into why.

Server Network: 105 Countries vs. Roughly 30

This is the widest gap between the two, and it's not close.

ExpressVPN operates servers in 105 countries — somewhere north of 3,000 machines total. That matters more than the raw number suggests. If you need to appear in Vietnam, Nigeria, or Uruguay for a market research project, ExpressVPN has a node. If you're testing how a landing page renders for customers in Turkey, same deal.

Norton Secure VPN covers 30+ countries. For everyday use — unblocking a US Netflix library, hiding your traffic on hotel Wi-Fi, checking a competitor's regional pricing — that's plenty. For anyone doing international work or regularly needing less common locations, it's a real limitation.

One more thing worth knowing: ExpressVPN's servers run entirely on RAM. Nothing is written to disk, so a server reboot wipes everything. Every provider claims something like this; ExpressVPN actually publishes the architecture behind it, which is why it shows up in so many third-party audits.

Speed and Protocols

Speed is where ExpressVPN has built its reputation, and it holds up in testing. Its proprietary Lightway protocol was open-sourced in 2023, which means independent researchers can inspect it — a move most competitors still haven't matched. Lightway connects fast (under a second in most tests) and holds up well on flaky connections, which is exactly what you want when you're switching networks between coffee shop and office.

ExpressVPN also added post-quantum protection to Lightway, which is forward-looking but genuinely matters if you handle anything sensitive and expect to keep it private for years.

Norton uses standard WireGuard and OpenVPN. There's nothing wrong with either — WireGuard is fast and modern, OpenVPN is battle-tested. But Norton's implementation sits in the middle of the pack. On a 300 Mbps home connection, I consistently saw ExpressVPN deliver more of the raw speed, and Norton lose a bit more on distant servers.

For browsing, email, and video calls, the difference is invisible. For large file transfers or 4K streaming, you'll notice it.
