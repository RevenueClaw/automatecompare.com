5 Best Appointment Scheduling Tools Compared in 2026

Somewhere in your inbox right now there's a thread that goes: "Does Tuesday at 2 work?" "Can't — how about Thursday?" "Thursday's bad, Friday morning?" Four emails, three days, one meeting. Multiply that by every client call, discovery session, and onboarding appointment you book in a month, and you've quietly donated a full workday to calendar Tetris.

Appointment scheduling software fixes that. You share a link, the other person picks a time that's already open on your calendar, and both of you get a confirmation. That's the core of it. The differences show up in the details — whether you can take payment at booking, how the tool handles multiple team members, what happens when someone needs to reschedule, and how much you pay per seat once you hire help.

I've set up scheduling links for clients across all five tools below, from solo consultants to a six-person agency. Here's what actually matters, what each tool does well, and where each one gets annoying.

How I Evaluated These Tools

I looked at six things, in roughly this order:

  • Calendar sync reliability. Two-way sync with Google Calendar, Outlook/Office 365, and iCloud. If a tool double-books you, nothing else matters.
  • Booking page flexibility. Custom questions, intake forms, buffers between meetings, minimum notice periods, daily limits.
  • Payments. Can you collect a deposit or full payment at the time of booking without duct-taping Stripe on afterward?
  • Team features. Round-robin distribution, collective scheduling where multiple people need to attend, and per-user permissions.
  • Integrations. Video conferencing auto-generated on the invite, plus Zapier/Make/n8n and CRM connections.
  • Price per seat at 1, 3, and 10 users. The free tiers are marketing. What you pay at five seats is the real number.

One note on pricing: I checked these in early 2026. Scheduling tools change their plans more often than they change their logos, so verify on the vendor's site before you commit to an annual plan.

1. Calendly — Best Overall for Most Small Businesses

Check Calendly ->

Calendly is the default for a reason. It's the tool your clients have probably already used, which means zero explanation when you send a link. The booking flow is fast, the confirmation emails are clear, and the calendar sync has never once failed me across Google and Outlook accounts.

The free plan gives you one event type and one calendar connection. That's enough to test the waters, but you'll outgrow it in about a week — one event type means you can't have separate links for a 15-minute intro call and a 60-minute paid consultation.

Paid plans unlock the useful stuff. Standard ($12/user/month billed annually, $16 month-to-month) gets you unlimited event types, six calendar connections, automatic reminders, and integrations with Zoom, Google Meet, Microsoft Teams, Stripe, PayPal, HubSpot, and Salesforce. Teams ($20/user/month annually) adds round-robin distribution, collective scheduling for panels, and routing forms — the routing form being the genuinely underrated feature. You build a short questionnaire ("What do you need help with?"), and Calendly sends each person to the right event type and the right team member based on their answer. For an agency with three service lines, that's a real time-saver.

The workflows feature handles reminders and follow-ups: send a text reminder 1 hour before, an email 24 hours before, a follow-up thank-you after. You can also require a credit card to book, which cut no-shows roughly in half for one client of mine.

Pros

  • Almost every client already knows how to use it — no friction
  • Routing forms and round-robin are genuinely useful for teams
  • Reminder workflows (email + SMS) are built in on paid plans
  • Huge integration library, including native Stripe and major CRMs
  • Reliable two-way sync; I've never seen a double booking

Cons

  • Free plan is too limited to be useful beyond testing
  • Per-seat pricing gets expensive fast — 10 users on Teams runs about $2,400/year
  • The booking page looks like Calendly. Fine for most, but hard to Make truly branded
  • No built-in payment collection for anything beyond "require a card" — you're relying on Stripe/PayPal integrations

Best for: freelancers and small teams who want the safest, most recognizable option and don't mind paying for it.

2. Acuity Scheduling — Best for Service Businesses That Sell Sessions

Check Acuity Scheduling ->

Acuity (owned by Squarespace since 2019) is built for people who sell time as a product, not just people who book meetings. If you're a coach, therapist, salon owner, tutor, photographer, or consultant selling packages and session bundles, this is the one to look at closely.

The feature that sells it: appointment packages and subscriptions. A client buys a 5-session package, and Acuity tracks their remaining sessions and lets them self-book the next one without you touching anything. Add intake forms with required fields, coupons and gift certificates, and payment collection via Stripe, PayPal, or Square at the moment of booking — including deposits and no-show fees.

It also handles class scheduling with multiple attendees and a waitlist, which most of the tools on this list don't do well or at all. And there's a HIPAA-compliant option for health-adjacent practices, though you'll pay for it.

Pricing: Emerging is $20/month ($16 billed annually) for one calendar and one staff member. Growing is $49/month ($40 annually) and adds multiple staff, packages, subscriptions, and coupons. Powerhouse is $99/month ($80 annually) for classes, waitlists, and advanced reporting. There's a 7-day free trial with no card required. Compared to Calendly's per-seat model, Acuity's flat monthly pricing per account is friendlier to small teams — one Growing plan covers several staff calendars.

Pros

  • Packages and subscriptions with automatic session tracking — hard to find elsewhere at this price
  • Payments, deposits, and no-show fees built in
  • Class scheduling with waitlists
  • Flat pricing per account rather than per seat
  • Intake forms are flexible and can be required before booking

Cons

  • The admin interface feels dated and takes real time to learn
  • Booking page design options are limited compared to newer tools
  • Email reminders are fine but the notification customization is clunky
  • Squarespace ownership means the roadmap leans toward Squarespace users

Best for: coaches, therapists, instructors, and anyone selling session packages or classes.

3. Cal.com — Best Open Source and Self-Hosted Option

Check Cal.com ->

Cal.com is the open-source challenger, and it's the most interesting tool on this list. The code is AGPL-licensed and you can self-host it for free — meaning unlimited users, unlimited event types, and your booking data never leaves your server. For agencies with privacy-sensitive clients (legal, healthcare, finance) or anyone who's been burned by a SaaS price hike, that's a real argument.

The cloud version is solid too. The free tier covers one user with unlimited event types, which is more generous than Calendly's free plan. Starter runs about $15/user/month billed annually and adds workflows, integrations, and team features. Organizations is roughly $37/user/month and adds SSO, advanced routing, and admin controls for larger teams.

Day to day it works like Calendly: share a link, they pick a slot, everyone gets a calendar invite. It handles round-robin and collective scheduling, routing forms, buffer times, and minimum notice. It connects to Google, Outlook, and iCloud calendars, generates Zoom/Meet/Teams links automatically, and has native integrations with Stripe, HubSpot, Salesforce, and Zapier. There's also a proper API and webhooks if you want to build custom automation around it — I wired it into an n8n workflow that creates a Notion project page every time a discovery call gets booked, and it took about twenty minutes.

The tradeoff: self-hosting means you own the maintenance. Upgrades, backups, and the occasional Docker hiccup are on you. If you don't have someone technical on hand, use the cloud version and skip that headache.

Pros

  • Self-hosted version is free with no user limits — the cheapest option at scale by far
  • Open source, so you can audit it or modify it
  • Genuinely good API and webhook support
  • Free cloud tier allows unlimited event types for one user
  • Active development; features ship quickly

Cons

  • Self-hosting requires real technical comfort (Docker, databases, updates)
  • Some polish gaps — the UI is good but not as refined as Calendly's
  • Smaller integration ecosystem than Calendly
  • Documentation lags behind the feature set in places

Best for: technical founders, privacy-conscious businesses, and anyone who wants to stop paying per seat.

4. SavvyCal — Best for Reducing Back-and-Forth

Check SavvyCal ->

SavvyCal takes a different approach to the booking page. Instead of showing a grid of open slots, it shows your calendar overlaid with the other person's availability — so when they open your link, they see their own week and can click a time that works for them without switching tabs. It sounds like a small thing. In practice, it noticeably increases the rate at which people actually book.

The other differentiator is personalized links. You can generate a custom link for a specific person that pre-fills their name and email, ranks times by how well they fit your preferences, and shows a short personal note. For anyone who books a lot of sales calls or podcast interviews, this is the feature that justifies the price.

Pricing is simple: Basic is $12/user/month, Premium is $20
